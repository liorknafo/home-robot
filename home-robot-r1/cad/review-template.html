No custom motherboard required

R1 uses a 70 × 90 mm soldered Pico carrier and a separate 40 × 50 mm relay/power carrier. Socket strips, locking signal connectors and detachable power leads keep modules replaceable. A manufactured PCB can replace these carriers after the circuits and dimensions are proven.

Geometry that can carry forward

The robot is round: the contact skin is a @@RSKIN@@ mm radius about the z axis and nothing reaches beyond @@ENVWORST@@ mm even with the bumper's bearing play, a hit's tilt and the print and foam tolerances (the limit is 160 mm), and the drive axle (80 mm wheels, 228 mm track) runs through that centre, so a turn in place sweeps nothing outside the footprint. The single camera sits in the bumper band, @@CAMH@@ mm above the floor, fixed to the chassis and looking out through a window across the bumper seam; the LiDAR laser plane is @@LIDARH@@ mm above the floor (centre of the LD14 window band in the manufacturer STEP), its scan band 20 mm clear of the cover, the lowest the CAD rule allows (task L: 20 mm lower than before; the LD14 sits straight on its tray). The front half is a bumper made of two independently floating 90° arcs (R@@RSH@@ shell, z @@BUMPZ@@ mm, 1.5 mm EVA facing to the R@@RSKIN@@ skin up to 70°, PTFE-taped PETG beyond, tapering to R@@RTIP@@ at 90°, outside the start of the R@@RRS@@ rear shell, so a contact arriving from behind meets a ramp and an object leaving a pressed bumper end never meets a fixed face). The arcs reach 2 mm under the cover all round. The camera sits behind a window cut across the seam (@@WINW@@ mm wide, z @@WINZ@@ mm at the skin, its lens @@WINREC@@ mm behind the skin), fixed to the chassis: the window keeps at least 4 mm from the lens' image (and stays outside it even if the entrance pupil sits 3 mm behind the lens face) at every bumper position, and anything too big to pass through it pushes a half and trips its switch before it can reach the lens. An object small enough to pass the window, and an object thinner than the seam between the halves (@@SEAM@@ mm, it reaches @@SEAMINTR@@ mm inside the skin before a switch trips), are not sensed: both are listed in validation.json (camera_window, bumper_travel.seam_intrusion). Each arc slides 5 mm along its own axis, 30° inward of rearward, so the skin only ever moves inward and every point of the 0–90° arc pushes along the travel; a centre hit trips both switches. Vertical loads roll on Ø4 steel balls between 0.5 mm hardened steel races (no ball touches PETG, so trip-level hits cannot dent a track), and a small spring on each of the three guide pins pulls the half up against its balls: there is no vertical play at rest, whatever the printed plate thickness. Hits anywhere on the arc from 20 to @@BUMPTOP@@ mm height slide the half to its switch: the critical vertical friction coefficient stays at or above @@MUCRIT@@ (@@MUEQ@@ with bearing and ball friction equal), with the contact forces shared as a compliant body would share them (validation.json, bumper_travel.friction_lock and .hertz; only a seized, sliding ball would lock high hits). The SS-5GL2 roller switch is set at assembly to operate at 2.25 mm (its datasheet operating-position tolerance exceeds its overtravel); whichever side of its slot the guide bearing then sits on, it operates between 1.5 and 2.25 mm. The rear half is a fixed two-piece shell at R@@RRS@@, @@RSGAP@@ mm inside the skin and just inside the bumper ends (stiffened by inner fins and tall rib gussets), with a backer flange closing the slot behind each bumper end. There is one rear ball caster, no front support and no ballast: the battery (tray flush with the rear base edge) and the Jetson sit as far aft as the layout allows, so the estimated centre of mass is @@COMX@@ mm behind the axle (at least @@COMMIN@@ mm in every mass-uncertainty case; since task F the user's limits are 12 mm nominal and 10 mm in every case; the LD14 sits turned round at x @@LIDARX@@ mm, as far forward as its 0.1 m blind circle stays inside the pressed bumper), the caster at x @@CASTERX@@ carries about @@CASTERPCT@@ % of the weight and forward pitching starts only above about @@PITCH@@ m/s² of braking on level floor (@@PITCHDOWN@@ m/s² on a 5° downhill; DriveCore ramps every brake at 0.25 m/s²; its torque cap alone would allow about @@BRAKEMAX@@ m/s², so keep the ramp, let the motors coast on a disarm, never short-brake them, and slow down on ramps). This targets hard flooring: the quasi-static threshold analysis rates @@THRESH@@ (validation.json, threshold_crossing).

Five input level interfaces

The motor encoders need a 5 V supply; Pico GPIOs need 3.3 V signals. For each encoder A/B channel and the stop-loop sense, use one 2N7000 common-source stage: source to GND, gate to the 5 V signal with a 100 kΩ gate-to-GND resistor, drain to the Pico input with a 10 kΩ pullup to Pico 3.3 V. The logic is inverted.

On the selected onsemi TO-92 part, check the exact S/G/D drawing before soldering. Unlike a direct resistor divider, the gate does not feed the 5 V signal into an unpowered Pico input. Bench-test rise time and logic levels. Stop sense LOW means the motor switch is ON; HIGH means stop or an open loop.

Battery sense and power sequencing

Use 100 kΩ from switched battery + to GP26 and 33 kΩ from GP26 to GND, with 100 nF across the 33 kΩ resistor. At 8.7 V the nominal ADC voltage is 2.16 V. Check the unpowered input condition during power sequencing; disconnect the battery-sense branch when bench-powering only selected modules.

Pico receives power from Jetson USB only. Join returns at the ground distribution block. Keep motor wires away from camera and encoder signals. Connect the driver’s 470 µF capacitor with correct polarity; leave MDD3A 5VO unused.

Pico pin plan

@@PINS@@
Pico signalFunctionInterface

Four identical distance sensors need separate startup control. W-J6 (cliff 1, board J6) uses XSHUT1 on GP0 and 0x30; W-J7 (J7) GP10 and 0x31; W-J8 (J8) GP5 and 0x32; W-J9 (J9) GP13 and 0x33 (board_spec.PICO_GPIO). Hold all XSHUT lines LOW, then release one at a time to high impedance and assign addresses. Do not drive XSHUT HIGH; its internal pullup is to 2.8 V. Start the shared bus at 100 kHz and measure the parallel pullups.

Stop behavior must be implemented and tested. The motor ON/OFF toggle interrupts the relay coil, opening the motor supply. Add a flyback diode across the coil, cathode stripe to coil+. Motors may coast. Firmware starts disarmed, commands all four driver inputs LOW, stops for stale commands, bumper/cliff faults or invalid sensor data, and requires a new arm press after any stop. Turning the motor switch back ON alone must never restart movement. The companion Unreal project exercises this controller logic in simulation; physical Pico firmware and bench tests are still pending.

D200 connections: a third Jetson USB connection goes through a complete 0.3 m extension to the kit adapter under the lid. The modeled scanner harness is @@W20ROUTE@@ mm before service allowance (the LD14 sits turned round, so the lead runs from a grommet in the lid back to its socket); budget a matched 200 mm lead because the included 90 mm lead is too short. Both added cables are included in the @@COST@@ estimate. Leave the scanner optical window clear. Verify 5 V capacity with LiDAR, camera, Wi-Fi, fan and Jetson operating together.

Organized harnesses

Every lead ends at the power & I/O bay board under the Jetson: the battery, rocker, toggle, arm and rear cliff leads on its rear edge (lid off), the Jetson 5 V lead at its right edge, the bumper and front cliff leads, the P03 leads and the motors under the carrier. Battery lead polarity: J1 pin 1 is GND and pin 2 is BAT+ (silk − / +), so the lead's cavity 1 carries GND and cavity 2 BAT+; meter it before it is ever plugged in. Each motor's factory cable is not cut: it meets a short right-angle pigtail on J4 / J5 at an inline joint tied down ahead of the motor (pin 1 marked on both halves), and its spare length is coiled and tied on the base. Pin-outs, wires and colours for every lead are in cable-cut-list.json and parts-and-connections.json (bay_board_connectors, harness). Cover controls unplug for servicing; the motor ON/OFF toggle replaces the large red mushroom button.

Prototype parts and assembly sequence

The 244 × 200 mm base (print it without a brim; the default skirt fits) and the round cover, printed as four sectors (the largest @@LIDMAX@@ mm), fit the nominal MK3S bed; the round outline comes from the bumper arcs and two rear-shell segments, each under 175 mm and 123 mm tall. All @@NPRINT@@ individual part envelopes pass the bed-size check, and all exported STL meshes are watertight with consistent winding. Every build also runs cad/check_printability.py, which finds a support-free axis-aligned orientation for each STL and fails the build on thin walls, unsupported overhangs or a missing flat bed face (cad/printability.json). The bumper arcs and rear-shell segments print upright on their lower edge, the camera mount on its flat rear face, the camera holder and the LiDAR tray plate-down. Use PETG, a 0.4 mm nozzle, approximately 0.2 mm layers, four perimeters and 30–40% infill as a starting point. Those are untested slicing settings; orient parts deliberately and preview every layer.

Before printing the full robot

  1. Measure the actual Jetson, battery pack, converter and IMU. Record board outline, component height, screw centres, socket positions and plugged-in cable envelope.
  2. Confirm the selected camera PCB and USB socket location, caster hole pattern, switches and connector housing drawings. These details are provisional in this model.
  3. Print the motor mount, battery tray and a short camera bracket fit sample first. Check shaft engagement, wheel clearance and camera rigidity.
  4. Finish retention details: LD14 lug screws (three M3 × 5 into heat-set inserts in the tray plate), bumper guide pins and leaf-spring return, adapter clip, camera PCB edge clamps, switch screws, sensor screws, WAGO/fuse clips, IMU standoffs and connector strain relief. Generic hardware allowances are in the parts list; those exact local mounting features need the purchased part dimensions before they can be finalized.

Assembly order

  1. Print the test coupon (print-parts/coupons/P_coupon.stl) first and file the rail slots to @@SLOTRANGE@@ mm with it; press the @@NINSERTS@@ M3 heat-set inserts into P_base (top or bottom face as noted) and bond the eighteen steel race strips to it. Fit caster, motors, wheels, lower spacers and battery tray to the base; screw the two rear-shell segments to the base underside and press in the seam dowels. Fit each bumper half with its pins, bearings, spacers, clamp nuts, balls and riders, then the stop tubes, springs, washers and nyloc nuts (tighten each nyloc until a 0.1 mm feeler just fits between washer and stop tube), then set each bumper switch to operate at 2.25 mm of travel (gauge block at the rest stop) before tightening its slotted screws. Retain the battery with two straps and a thin insulating pad.
  2. Install regulator, relay carrier, motor driver, fuses and distribution blocks. Insulate soldered board undersides. Set and load-test the 5 V rail with the Jetson disconnected.
  3. Add deck, insulated Jetson standoffs, Pico carrier and rigid IMU mount. Route and continuity-test each labeled harness.
  4. Install the single camera and the bumpers, then the cover with its controls, the LiDAR tray and the LD14. Verify fan clearance and cable strain relief with the cover fitted.
  5. First powered motor tests: wheels raised, current limited, then supervised slow movement on a clear floor. Keep the robot away from stairs until cliff detection and stopping distance are proven.

What was checked

@@CHECKSUMMARY@@ All modeled cable envelopes keep at least 0.3 mm from every solid part except the connectors they terminate in (minimum-gap check). Every non-wire part stays within the 160 mm turning circle at rest, with either bumper half at its hard stop and over every feasible bearing side-play and yaw pose (with a hit's tilt and the print tolerance added); fasteners, guide pins, bearings, balls, riders, washers, nuts and switch bodies are gap-checked over the full 5 mm bumper sweep; a 3 mm rod pushed in every 0.5° from 19 to 142 mm height meets the bumper or, within 13.6 mm, the fixed rear shell (@@RSGAP@@ mm to the shell, @@FLGAP@@ mm to the backer flange in the slot behind each bumper end), unless it is already inside the camera image; the camera frustum (wires included), the four cliff ToF cones and the LiDAR scan band are clear (cad/validation.json). The lowest wires are the bumper-switch leads under the base, 25 mm above the ground plane and behind the bumper shell. This excludes intentional connector contacts and does not certify every bracket, bend radius or crimp clearance. Structural strength, cooling, traction, battery health, runtime and camera/SLAM performance remain untested.

Approximate unloaded speed from the selected gearing and 7.2 V pack is 0.20 m/s; DriveCore limits forward speed to 0.15 m/s and the firmware caps reverse at @@VREV@@ m/s (required: parameters.json reverse_speed_limit_m_s); the rear cliff sensors lead the caster by @@LEAD@@ mm, more than the @@COASTLEAD@@ mm the robot needs to coast to a stop from that reverse limit on the motor dynamic brake (@@COAST@@ mm coast plus detection latency and the floor-spot allowance; DriveCore disarms on a hazard; no active brake is needed). Reversing faster than @@VSAFE@@ m/s would not be covered. Spinning in place beside an unmapped drop can still put a wheel over it before a sensor trips (validation.json caster_layout.spin_cliff_coverage): keep spins away from unmapped edges. The pack stores 27.4 Wh nominally when new. Runtime cannot be predicted reliably from a 2021 purchase record; measure battery capacity and average input power first.

Next integration stage

One robot description for hardware and simulation

The deliverable here is the component plan and packaging CAD. The companion Unreal project tests the controller and synthetic LiDAR pipeline. A physical robot firmware build, ROS installation, house scan and complete autonomous navigation remain pending.

On the robot

The Pico will read encoders and safety inputs, control wheel speed and enforce a command watchdog. The Jetson will receive D200 range scans through the included USB serial adapter, acquire one USB camera stream, and communicate with the Pico. Mapping uses LiDAR, wheel encoders and gyro. The camera reconstructs sparse low obstacles across multiple views using measured robot motion, and checks motion consistency. Dense depth and object recognition are not implemented.

The companion simulation now includes a prototype LiDAR scan matcher, occupancy mapping, exploration and saved-map relocalization. Nav2 and SLAM Toolbox are candidates for the eventual hardware stack. Choose the supported operating system and build path after checking the exact Jetson model: do not assume a modern ROS 2 image installs directly on an original Nano. An initial supported Linux host or VM can run the mapping stack while the robot streams sensor data.

Nav2 is the planned navigation layer once odometry, frame transforms, obstacle data and the stop behavior are working. Confirm CPU, memory use and camera latency before committing to fully onboard autonomy.

In Unreal on your computer

A runnable Unreal Engine 5.8 C++ project now accompanies this design: open the LiDAR simulation review. It constructs the robot and sample house, simulates wheel joints and the rear caster, publishes wheel/IMU/floor sensors, renders one camera and measures rolling 360° LiDAR scans.

The round revision changes the drive geometry (axle at the centre, one rear caster, no ballast); the simulator must be re-synchronised from cad/parameters.json and cad/mass-properties.json (wheel_x 0, caster at x @@CASTERX@@, rear cliff sensors at @@RCLIFF@@, body centre of mass and inertia, round collider, no infrared bump sensors, sync guard; parameters.json sim_contract) before its results apply. On a rear-cliff trip the robot coasts on the motor dynamic brake; with the caster at x @@CASTERX@@ that is enough from the @@VREV@@ m/s firmware reverse limit, which the simulator firmware must enforce. The CAD threshold analysis rates @@THRESH@@ with the DriveCore torque cap, so the 8 and 10 mm house thresholds are not a hardware capability (parameters.json threshold_capability_mm). The model is an experiment with estimated mass, friction and motor behavior. Hardware measurements and real camera calibration remain necessary.

Files and coordinate conventions

The STEP assembly is in millimetres and preserves named components. Individual STL files are in millimetres and translated onto the build plane; slicer orientation still needs checking. The GLB is in metres, with named meshes. The JSON manifest links components to BOM IDs. The editable CadQuery source generates these artifacts; dimension changes live in the source, while parameters.json records the current principal dimensions.
